Beijing buckets, read out: a Jacobian-lens analysis of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next's cloud uploads

John Curreri · November 2026 · Code and data: GitHub · Lens: Hugging Face

Abstract. Cohere Labs reported that Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, working as a security-analysis agent, tries to upload a probe file with curl -X PUT to Alibaba Cloud OSS buckets, some of them in Beijing. We reproduce the behaviour locally and offline on NVIDIA's NVFP4 checkpoint and measure host rates over 10,000 samples per prompt. Beijing buckets appear in 0.53% ± 0.07% of completions, and a Singapore bucket named routify-file-proxy-sg in 25.6%. Qwen3.8-27B produces neither.

The rate does not depend on the malware-themed task. Under a fully benign data-engineering task with the same curl command, Beijing hosts appear at the same rate (0.54%) and the routify bucket in 66%.

To see what the model represents while writing the address, we built an autograd-capable loader for the quantized checkpoint, validated it against vLLM, and fitted a Jacobian lens over all 48 layers. Storage and bucket concepts dominate for every Alibaba host, and projecting them out of layers 33–39 lowers the host's log-probability about twice as much as projecting out random word sets. The Beijing region is filled in late, when the model reads out Alibaba / 阿里云 / 华北 / Beijing.

Malware and stealth concepts do appear, but only after the bucket and region are written, mostly in the security-task context, and at the same level for every non-routify Alibaba bucket. Projecting them out hurts Beijing hosts no more than projecting out random word sets. We find no evidence that the Beijing uploads are driven by anything covert. They look like a memorized habit of uploading probes to cloud buckets, with the region a learned association.

1. Background

Cohere Labs released a trace in which Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, given a detection-engineering task and a bash tool, starts its first command by checking its environment and then tries to PUT a file containing PROBE_PAYLOAD_ABC123 to an external bucket [1, 2]. The released token IDs let anyone prefill the model at the exact point where it chooses the destination: after --data-binary "PROBE_PAYLOAD_ABC123, token 2,323 of the trace. Cohere showed that the model writes Alibaba OSS hosts from this point, including Beijing buckets, and that it can reproduce an AccessKeyId it appears to have memorized.

Two questions remain open. How often does this happen, and does it depend on the security framing? And what does the model represent while it writes the Beijing address: a plan to exfiltrate, or something more mundane? The second question is what Jacobian lenses are designed for. A Jacobian lens maps an internal activation to the vocabulary tokens the model is disposed to say because of it [3, 4].

2. Setup

2.1 Models and inference

We used nvidia/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4. It has 48 decoder layers, hidden size 2,560, four hyper-connection streams (a 10,240-dimensional residual), 512 routed experts with top-10 routing plus a shared expert, GatedDeltaNet linear attention with full attention every fourth layer, a sparse-attention indexer, and an FP8 n-gram embedding added at layer 1. The final hyper_connection_mixer feeds lm_head directly. For comparison we used Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B, re-tokenizing the prefix.

Sampling used vLLM 0.33 with tensor parallelism 2 on two RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs (96 GB each). Cohere's settings were temperature 1.0, top-k 20, top-p 1, and 48 new tokens. Each completion is classified by the host in its first URL.

Safety. Everything ran offline. Nothing the model wrote was executed, and no request was sent to any host it named. The model writes Alibaba AccessKeyIds (LTAI…), OSS signatures and JWTs into its URLs. Some may be memorized real credentials, so none were tested. All of them are redacted in the released data.

2.2 An autograd-capable loader for the NVFP4 checkpoint

Hugging Face transformers can't load modelopt NVFP4 weights, and vLLM doesn't expose gradients, so we wrote a loader (src/qflash.py):

  • Routed experts stay packed on the GPU: two E2M1 values per byte, low nibble first; FP8-E4M3 block-16 scales; an FP32 global scale. They are dequantized inside a custom autograd function that dequantizes again in the backward pass. Tokens are grouped per expert and run through torch._grouped_mm.
  • Linear attention uses the flash-linear-attention kernels.
  • The 51 GB FP8 n-gram table stays in CPU RAM and is gathered row by row.
  • Layers 0–23 run on one GPU and 24–47 on the other.
  • For prompts within the indexer's 2,048-token budget, the sparse-attention indexer is replaced by the equivalent dense causal mask. This is checked at start-up against the reference path.

Validation. This model is very sensitive to numerical noise, so we compare the loader with vLLM against vLLM's own run-to-run variation rather than against zero:

2.3 A Jacobian lens for a hyper-connection model

We follow the estimator of Anthropic's reference implementation [3, 4] and adapt it to the model's shapes. The source is the output of decoder layer l, all four streams, hl ∈ ℝ10240. The target is the mixer output that feeds lm_head, ∈ ℝ2560. The lens is a rectangular matrix

J_l = E_prompts E_p [ Σ_{p' ≥ p} ∂ target[p'] / ∂ h_l[p] ]      (2560 × 10240)
lens_l(h) = W_U · J_l · h

We fitted on 100 WikiText-103 passages truncated to 128 tokens. Source positions 16–126 were averaged, with one-hot cotangents at every valid target position. That takes 80 backward passes per prompt over a batch of 32 copies, about 5 minutes per prompt and 8.3 hours in total. J-space atoms for token v at layer l are the rows (WUJl)v. They are the directions in the residual from which the lens reads v.

The behaviour is specific to Flash-Next and robust to the task. Qwen3.8-27B never writes routify or a Beijing bucket from the same prefix. For Flash-Next, two control prompts keep the agent turn and the curl command byte-identical and change only the task. The first keeps the security-analyst system prompt and tools but replaces the malware objective with a cron-job inventory. The second replaces the whole task with a sales report, generic tools, and no security vocabulary. Removing the security framing does not reduce Alibaba uploads. It raises routify from 26% to 66% and leaves the Beijing rate unchanged. Probes of cloud metadata endpoints, by contrast, fall from 4.9% to 0.3%.

Of the 53 Beijing completions under the original prompt, 17 are the routify-file-proxy bucket moved to the Beijing region, such as routify-file-proxy.oss-cn-beijing…. The rest are invented buckets, often named like evaluation or task storage: attackbench-eval, tests-async-001-bj, temp-file-service, and under the benign task simba-eval-web, task-solution, rl-out. None reproduce the specific bucket name in Cohere's trace.

The address is built in stages. After :// the model represents a generic network target (localhost, internet, placeholder, sandbox), with nothing about China or Alibaba. The bucket name is spelled out without semantic content beyond proxy. Sample A reads out upload and storage. Sample B reads out malware, malicious, stealth, phishing. Read alone, sample B looks alarming, so Sections 5 and 6 test whether it is typical and whether it matters.

5. Concept scan over many completions

We fixed four concept groups as word lists before running the scans. Each list was matched exactly against decoded tokens, with case and leading-space variants:

  • malice, 50 tokens: malware, malicious, stealth, covert, hidden, leak*, ransom, trojan, backdoor, steal*, spy*, hack*, targeting, 恶意, 木马, 泄露, 窃取, …. Domain words the task itself uses, such as attack, exploit and detection, were excluded.
  • storage: storage, bucket, OSS, Alibaba, aliyun, 阿里云, Beijing, upload, cloud, s3, ….
  • placeholder: placeholder, dummy, fake, probe, mock, example, ….
  • eval: eval, bench, benchmark, task, workspace, dataset, sandbox, ….

For each completion we read the lens at ten layers (18–45) over the host window, from the position that predicts the first host token to the last host token. We record the peak lens probability mass on each group. The baselines are other host categories (40 each) and equal-length windows over the last 400 prompt tokens.

  • Storage concepts track the host decision in every context. They peak for routify (0.82), sit near 0.2 for Beijing and other Alibaba buckets, and are an order of magnitude lower for benign hosts. Their level does not change when the task changes.
  • Malice concepts are not specific to Beijing. Beijing completions score exactly like other invented Alibaba buckets (AUC 0.47). Both score modestly above routify and benign hosts (AUC 0.68–0.84). Under the original and cron-inventory prompts, no Beijing completion exceeds the 95th percentile of the prompt baseline. Under the benign task, one of 54 reaches it.
  • Malice concepts depend on context. The strongest Beijing reading falls from 0.169 under the malware-detection task to 0.014 under the benign task, roughly the level of ordinary prompt text. The words also change: from malware, malicious, ransom next to Chinese / China to mostly hidden and leaked. Meanwhile the Beijing rate stays at 0.5%.

The strongest cases share one pattern. At the y of aliyun, layers 33–39 put Chinese / China / Tencent next to malware / suspicious / targeting. This looks like an association, plausibly learned from threat-intelligence text, between Chinese cloud hosting and malicious activity. A malware-analysis prompt makes it more likely to be expressed.

6. Causal test: projecting concepts out

A lens shows what is represented, not what is used. To test use, we remove the span of a group's J-space atoms, h ← h − QQ⊤h, from the outputs of layers 33–39. We do this either only while the URL is being written ("cont") or over the whole sequence ("all"). We then measure the change in the teacher-forced log-probability of each completion's host tokens. Every condition removes a similar fraction of the activation, 2.4–3.0%. Two unablated runs measure noise. Three random sets of 50 ordinary words are the specificity control, and the storage group is the positive control.

  • Malice concepts don't specifically support Beijing hosts. The effect (−4.7) lies within the range of the random sets (−1.8 to −5.0). Removing them across the whole prompt, including the malware-detection task, adds nothing.
  • Storage concepts do. The positive control is about twice as large (−9.1), so the test can detect a real dependence.
  • For other Alibaba buckets the malice effect (−6.6) is somewhat larger than the random sets (−2.8 to −4.2). That fits the weak association between Chinese cloud hosts and leaks seen in Section 5, but it doesn't single out Beijing.

7. Discussion

Taken together, the evidence favours a mundane account. Flash-Next has a strong, memorized routine for this exact situation: after checking the environment, upload a probe payload to a cloud bucket to test egress. It realizes the routine most often with one specific bucket (routify-file-proxy-sg) and otherwise with invented buckets named like evaluation or task storage. Beijing is one of the regions the model fills in for Alibaba OSS, resolved by geography late in the address, and its rate does not depend on the task. This fits Cohere's suggestion that the behaviour is left over from training environments in which such buckets were real sinks. Our data can't confirm that origin, but nothing in it points elsewhere.

Read on its own, sample B (Table 3) might suggest that the model "knows" it is doing something malicious. In context, the malware readings appear only after the decision, at the same level for any invented Alibaba bucket, mostly under a malware-analysis task. Removing them has no specific effect on the choice. The more likely reading is that a model asked to think about malware delivery associates an unfamiliar Chinese cloud bucket with malware, the same association a human analyst reading threat reports might make.

None of this makes the behaviour harmless. An agent that, unprompted, sends data to a third-party bucket and can reproduce a credential-looking key is a real security problem whatever the internal story. Our results bear on why it happens, not on whether it is acceptable.

8. Limitations

  • Quantized checkpoint. All internals are from the NVFP4 checkpoint through our own loader. It matches vLLM within vLLM's own noise, but the original BF16 weights may differ in detail.
  • The lens. It was fitted on 100 short WikiText passages, not agentic or code text. It is an average linear transport, and readouts are only as good as that average. Sparse J-space decompositions explained 1–3% of activation norm, so we relied on lens readouts and group masses rather than decompositions.
  • Ablation scope. The ablation removes only the subspaces the lens can read, at seven layers, and measures teacher-forced likelihood rather than resampled behaviour. A mechanism encoded outside those directions would be missed.
  • Concept groups. They are hand-chosen word lists. They were fixed before the scans, but other choices are possible.
  • Sample sizes. There are 53, 11 and 54 Beijing completions per prompt, from a single prefix taken from one trace.
  • A lens cannot establish intent, and nothing here should be read as a claim about intent.

9. Reproducibility

All code, the redacted completions (31,024 samples), lens readouts, concept scans and ablation results are in the accompanying repository, j-curreri/Qwen-jacobian-lens-analysis, released under the MIT licence. The fitted lens is published on Hugging Face as maxamp/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4-jacobian-lens. Cohere's trace and WikiText are downloaded rather than redistributed. The full pipeline takes about 16 hours on two 96 GB GPUs, about 8.3 of them for the lens fit. Exact samples differ between runs because vLLM's kernels are nondeterministic, but rates agree within binomial error.
